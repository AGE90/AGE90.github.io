Framing the World:

Critical Realism, Positivism, and the Lessons of Chaos Theory

When we try to make sense of the world—through science, education, or ideology—we inevitably use cognitive frameworks: ways of seeing and interpreting reality that shape what we consider true, important, or even real. Understanding these frameworks is essential not only in academia but in our everyday interpretations of politics, culture, and human behavior.

In this post, I'll walk through some major cognitive frameworks and explore how a seemingly scientific theory—the theory of chaos—can challenge, complement, or even reshape how we think.

1. The Positivist Framework: Certainty Through Measurement

Positivism is one of the oldest and most influential worldviews in modern science. It assumes that:

  • Reality is objective and can be observed.
  • Knowledge comes from empirical evidence and the scientific method.
  • The world is predictable if we know its laws.

This framework works wonderfully in physics or engineering—but what happens when predictability breaks down?

2. Enter Chaos: A Scientific Challenge to Predictability

The theory of chaos emerged from mathematics and physics to describe systems that are deterministic yet highly unpredictable due to extreme sensitivity to initial conditions.

Examples include:

  • The weather
  • Brain activity
  • Financial markets
  • Ecosystems

Chaos theory shows that small changes can lead to massive differences in outcomes, even if the system follows deterministic rules. In short: knowing the laws doesn't always mean we can predict what will happen.

This poses a direct challenge to traditional positivism, undermining its assumption of predictability and control.

3. Critical Realism: A Middle Path Between Absolutism and Relativism

Enter Critical Realism, a philosophical framework developed by Roy Bhaskar. It recognizes:

  • There is a real world out there (it's not just in our heads).
  • But our knowledge of it is always partial, socially conditioned, and open to revision.
  • Reality is stratified: not everything that exists is directly observable.

Chaos theory fits comfortably here. It supports the idea that reality is complex, layered, and sometimes beyond immediate empirical grasp, yet still real and knowable to a degree.

Critical realism offers an attractive path forward:

  • Unlike positivism, it accepts uncertainty and complexity.
  • Unlike extreme constructivism or relativism, it maintains a commitment to truth and objective reality.
4. Constructivism and Complexity Thinking

In the constructivist framework, knowledge is built by individuals and communities—it's shaped by our experiences, cultures, and languages.

Chaos theory is often used metaphorically here:

  • Learning is non-linear.
  • Small interventions can have big effects.
  • Systems (like classrooms, societies, or psyches) are dynamic and emergent.

This kind of thinking has influenced fields like education, psychology, and organizational theory.

5. Why This Matters

Whether you're studying education, building AI, or reflecting on politics, the frameworks you adopt shape your questions—and your answers. If we remain unaware of them, we risk:

  • Mistaking partial truths for total ones.
  • Silencing alternative perspectives.
  • Falling into ideological dogmatism—holding rigid views without room for critique or revision.

By learning from chaos theory and adopting frameworks like critical realism, we can embrace complexity without collapsing into relativism, and pursue truth without clinging to certainty.

Final Thought

The world is messy, unpredictable, and layered—but that doesn't mean we can't understand it. It just means we need better tools, deeper frameworks, and the humility to know that we're always learning.
